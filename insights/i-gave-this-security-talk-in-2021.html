I Gave This Security Talk in 2021. Here’s What I Got Wrong.

By Michael Murphy

I found an old deck last week. A lunch-and-learn I delivered in 2021 called Cybersecurity and Your Company: ten tips for protecting a small business from cyber threats. Colonial Pipeline had just happened. Everyone was still working from their kitchen tables. I was making jokes about hackers having nothing better to do during lockdown.

I reread the whole thing, expecting to be mildly embarrassed by the design.

Instead I found something more useful. Some of that advice has aged remarkably well (better than I would have guessed), and in one case it’s since been formally adopted as federal guidance. Some of it is still technically true but no longer sufficient, which is the most dangerous category there is. And a couple of things I told that room, with real confidence, I would now consider malpractice to repeat.

One statistic in the deck was simply false. I’ll start there, because it’s mine to own.

So this isn’t a refresh. It’s an audit of my own advice, five years later. If you’re a business owner sitting on a security policy you wrote during the pandemic and haven’t opened since, this is the exercise I’d recommend you run on it.

The part I got wrong

On slide four, I told a room full of business owners that 60% of small companies go out of business within six months of a cyber attack.

That statistic is not true. It never was.

It traces back to the National Cyber Security Alliance around 2012, and in 2022 the NCSA confirmed it never produced the underlying data. Security researcher Adrian Sanabria has spent years tracking the claim to its source; his conclusion about how the number was derived is blunt: someone made it up. It’s still everywhere. It’s in vendor pitches, news segments, and at least one U.S. Senator’s op-ed. In 2021, it was in my slide deck, because I picked it up from a source I trusted and didn’t check it.

Here’s why I’m not just quietly deleting the slide.

That number was doing a specific job in my presentation: it was manufacturing fear so the ten tips that followed would feel urgent. That’s a sales technique, not an education technique, and I was doing it in a talk I’d introduced as education. The fact that I believed the statistic doesn’t get me all the way off the hook; I was in the business of knowing better.

The honest data is still bad enough to justify the talk. Verizon’s 2025 report put the share of small businesses facing bankruptcy after an attack at 19%. VikingCloud found that 40% of small businesses say a $100,000 incident would end them. IBM puts the average breach cost for a company under 500 employees in the low millions. One in five is a genuinely alarming number. It just isn’t three in five, and I don’t need to inflate it.

If you’re going to take one thing from this article, take that. Ask your IT provider where their scary number comes from. If they can’t tell you, that tells you something.

What aged well

Not everything needs replacing, and it’s worth being specific about what held, because it points at which kinds of advice are durable.

“Cybersecurity is a team sport.” I opened the tips with an argument that security starts with leadership, not the IT department. Five years later, that’s the most correct thing in the deck. Every framework that matters now (NIST CSF 2.0 added an entire Govern function in 2024) has moved in exactly that direction. Security outcomes turn out to be governance outcomes.

“Passwords should be like vacations: long and memorable.” I said the industry was moving away from 30-day forced rotation and toward long passwords you don’t change. That was a somewhat contrarian position to take in a room in 2021, where most people were still being nagged to change their password every 90 days.

It’s now the federal standard. NIST finalized Revision 4 of SP 800-63B in 2025: no mandatory periodic rotation unless there’s evidence of compromise, no composition rules, length over complexity with 15 characters recommended, and mandatory screening against known-breached password lists. If your firm still enforces 90-day resets with a symbol-and-a-number rule, you’re not being extra careful. You’re running a policy that current guidance specifically advises against, and you’re getting Password1! → Password2! for your trouble.

“Get help. Start with an assessment.” Still the right closing move. The example I used (an entertainment center storing thousands of Social Security numbers in Excel files on someone’s personal Dropbox, collected over years for prize reporting, with nobody left who remembered setting it up) is the single most representative story in the deck. That’s what assessments find. Not sophisticated attacks. Forgotten decisions.

The pattern in what held up: the advice about people and process aged well. The advice about specific technical controls aged badly. That’s not a coincidence, and it’s worth remembering the next time you’re deciding what to write down.

What aged badly

1. I taught people to spot phishing by looking for bad English

I spent three slides on a phishing example: a fake American Express security alert I’d actually received. I walked the room through the red flags: the sender domain was ucsd.edu instead of amex.com, the link pointed to amazonaws.com, and there was a typo, plus the wonderfully clunky phrase “you are recommended to kindly.”

Then I said this, verbatim: “It’s a good thing hackers typically don’t speak English as their first language.”

That tell is gone. It has been gone for about three years. Generative AI writes flawless, idiomatic, on-brand English at zero marginal cost, in any language, with the correct corporate tone, and it scales that across thousands of personalized messages. The grammatical error was never a security control in the first place. It was a lucky accident of attacker economics, and the economics changed.

Worse, I trained a room full of people to use it as a filter. Anyone who internalized my 2021 advice now has a heuristic that produces exactly the wrong answer: this email is well-written, so it’s probably legitimate.

The link-destination lesson survives. That part I got right, and it’s still the fastest check available. But the whole approach needs rebuilding around one idea: stop teaching people to detect fakes and start teaching them to verify through a second channel.

Detection is a race against a curve that’s moving away from you. Verification isn’t.

Also worth retiring: my “92.4% of malware is delivered via email” slide. Even setting aside that the figure is from a 2018 report, the framing puts a wall around the wrong thing. Phishing now arrives by text, by voice call, through Teams and Slack messages, through poisoned search results and fake browser update prompts. Email is one channel among several, and the ones that bypass your email gateway entirely are the ones growing fastest.

2. “It will always be verbal”: the worst advice in the deck

This is the one that genuinely bothers me.

I told that room to have an explicit conversation with staff: the owner will never ask you to do anything financial over email. No gift cards, no wire instructions, no account numbers. Those conversations will happen verbally, because email is too easy to impersonate.

In 2021, that was solid, practical guidance. Business email compromise was the dominant fraud, and moving the confirmation to voice genuinely defeated it.

Today, “I heard his voice” is not a control.

The case worth knowing is Arup, the engineering firm behind the structural work on the Sydney Opera House. In early 2024, a finance employee in their Hong Kong office joined a video call about a confidential acquisition. The CFO was on the call. So were several colleagues the employee recognized. Afterward, the employee executed fifteen wire transfers totaling $25.6 million. Every participant except the employee was an AI-generated deepfake: faces, voices, and mannerisms, all fabricated.

The source material for a voice clone is already public. Your LinkedIn video. Your podcast appearance. Last quarter’s webinar.

So the 2021 rule has to be rewritten, and it’s not a small edit; the fix is a different shape than the original:

  • Money moves on a verified callback, not on a conversation. The recipient hangs up and calls a number already on file, never a number provided during the request itself, and never a number in the email signature.
  • Any change to payment details is treated as an event, not as a routine update: written confirmation plus a callback plus a mandatory hold before funds release.
  • Two people approve anything above a dollar threshold you set today.
  • Urgency and confidentiality together are the signal. “Don’t discuss this with anyone, and it has to go today” is the shape of every one of these frauds. Say so out loud to your staff, and make clear nobody will ever be punished for slowing down a payment to verify it.

Notice what changed philosophically. My 2021 advice moved the trust to a channel I believed couldn’t be faked. My 2026 advice assumes every channel can be faked and builds verification that doesn’t depend on recognizing anyone.

3. MFA: still essential, no longer sufficient

Tip #3 was multi-factor authentication, and I made the case with a real screenshot: an executive’s mailbox we’d instrumented on a Friday, showing by Monday a global map of automated login attempts using his previously-breached credentials. That demonstration still works. I’d still show it.

But I left that room with the impression that MFA was the finish line, and in 2026 it’s the starting line.

The attacks moved to the space MFA doesn’t cover. Adversary-in-the-middle phishing kits relay you through the real login page, let you complete MFA legitimately, and steal the session token the identity provider issues afterward: no password needed, no second prompt triggered. Infostealer malware harvests those same session cookies straight off the endpoint. Push-bombing spams approval prompts until someone taps to make it stop. And the most effective version doesn’t involve any technology at all: the attacker calls your help desk, impersonates an employee, and asks to have MFA reset. That’s how Scattered Spider took down MGM and Caesars in 2023, and how the Marks & Spencer and Co-op intrusions started in 2025. From one phone call to domain admin, sometimes in under an hour, with no malware involved.

What I’d tell the room now:

  • Move privileged accounts to phishing-resistant MFA: passkeys, FIDO2 security keys, or certificate-based auth. These bind to the real site’s origin and simply will not authenticate against a spoofed one. NIST now formally recognizes passkeys, and ranks phishing-resistant authenticators above password-plus-MFA.
  • Get off SMS codes where you can. NIST has removed SMS one-time codes from the methods that satisfy its AAL2 assurance level. Use an app with number matching at minimum.
  • Write down your help desk identity verification procedure. Who can reset MFA, what proof is required, and what happens when someone calls in a panic claiming to be an executive. This is the control almost nobody has, and it’s the one currently being used against the largest companies in the world.
  • Watch the session, not just the login. Impossible travel, new device, token reuse from an unfamiliar network.

There’s a data point from Verizon’s 2026 report I find genuinely useful here: 73% of ransomware victims had an infostealer infection or credential-leak event in the year before the attack, and half of those occurred within 95 days of it. That reframes credential monitoring from housekeeping into an early warning system. A small organization sees a median of seven credential leak events a year. Those aren’t noise. They’re the countdown.

4. Backups solve the 2021 problem, not the 2026 one

Tip #4 was backup, backup, backup, and my argument was that if firms had good offline backups, they wouldn’t be paying these ransoms.

That worked. Genuinely, this is a defense that won. Verizon’s 2026 report shows 69% of ransomware victims now refuse to pay, up from 65% the year before, with the median payment down to roughly $140,000. Backup and recovery maturity is a big part of why.

Attackers noticed, and adapted. The modern playbook doesn’t just encrypt your data, it steals a copy first and threatens to publish it. Against that, a perfect backup restores your operations and does nothing at all about your exposure. You’re still making breach notifications. You’re still explaining to clients why their information is on a leak site. Ransomware appeared in 48% of breaches in the 2026 report, up from 44%, even as payments fell, and the tactics have shifted toward maximum operational disruption and data auctions precisely because straight encryption stopped paying.

So the tip stands, with additions: backups must be immutable (an attacker with domain admin cannot delete or encrypt them), tested with an actual restore on a date you can name, and paired with a plan for the disclosure half of the problem, which means knowing what sensitive data you hold and where, before you need to answer that question under deadline.

My original homework question was: when did you last test a file restore? That’s still exactly the right question, and I’d bet most readers still can’t answer it.

5. The patching tip needs a new villain

I spent tip #6 calling out Windows XP and Windows 7 machines still sitting in production. Same speech, new names: Windows 10 reached end of support on October 14, 2025. If you have Windows 10 endpoints running without Extended Security Updates, that’s today’s version of the machine I was yelling about in 2021.

But the bigger change is one I didn’t anticipate. In the 2026 Verizon data, exploitation of vulnerabilities overtook stolen credentials as the number one initial access vector for the first time in the report’s history (31% of breaches, up from 20%). And organizations are losing ground on remediation: only about a quarter of critical vulnerabilities were fully remediated in the past year, with median time to fix stretching to 43 days.

The sharpest detail: internet-facing edge devices and VPNs now account for 22% of vulnerability-exploitation breaches, up from 3% the year before. That’s a sevenfold increase in one year, and it lands directly on small businesses, because the firewall and VPN appliance sitting in the closet is exactly the device nobody has a patching process for. In 2021 I told you to inventory everything on your network and patch it systematically. I’d now say: start that inventory at the edge and work inward, and treat CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog as your priority queue rather than patching by severity score.

What wasn’t on the list in 2021 (and should be now)

Three things belong in this talk that never occurred to me to include.

Your vendors are now your attack surface. Third-party involvement appeared in 48% of breaches in the 2026 Verizon report, a 60% year-over-year increase. Your bookkeeper, your practice management SaaS, your marketing agency, and yes, your IT provider. I closed my 2021 talk by telling people to ask their vendors what they’re doing to protect their data, and I said it as a throwaway line. It should have been its own tip. Ask for their SOC 2 report, ask what happens to your data if they’re breached, and ask specifically about the vendors with standing administrative access into your systems.

Shadow AI is the new shadow IT. In 2021 the worry was staff signing up for unsanctioned Dropbox accounts. Today it’s client information pasted into free-tier AI tools, which is both wildly useful and completely invisible to you. I’ve written about that at length separately, but it belongs on any current list of the things quietly leaving your perimeter.

Cyber insurance moved from “buy it” to “prove it.” My original slide made the case that general liability doesn’t cover cyber incidents and you need a dedicated policy. Still true, and still news to a lot of owners.

What’s changed is the underwriting. Carriers now require MFA, EDR or managed detection, tested and immutable backups, and a written incident response plan, as conditions of coverage, not as suggestions. And they verify. In the Travelers case involving International Control Services, the insured had attested to MFA on remote access; the post-breach investigation found MFA on the firewall but not on the systems the attackers actually used, and the parties stipulated to rescinding the policy outright. Not a denied claim: a policy voided from inception, as if it had never existed.

So the tip is now: buy the policy, and then make sure every answer on the application is literally true of your whole environment. “We have MFA” needs to survive the question “on what, exactly?” That means the global admin account, the VPN, and the backup console too. An honest answer with a remediation plan beats an impressive one you can’t prove, every single time.

The actual lesson

Rereading my own deck, the thing that struck me hardest wasn’t any individual tip. It was that a document I would have described as basically evergreen had quietly gone stale in ways I couldn’t see from the inside.

Nothing in that presentation announced itself as out of date. There’s no error message. “Train your staff to spot phishing” still sounds correct. “Enable MFA” still sounds correct. “Confirm financial requests verbally” sounds especially correct. Every one of those sentences reads as true today. It’s the world behind them that moved, and the sentences stayed put.

That’s the failure mode worth guarding against: not the advice that’s obviously wrong, but the advice that’s still technically true and no longer sufficient. It doesn’t get revisited, because nothing about it looks broken.

So here’s my actual recommendation, and it’s the only one in this article I’m confident will still be right in 2031:

Put your security policy on a calendar. Once a year, read what you wrote, and for each control ask a different question than “is this still true?” Ask: what would an attacker do to get around this now? If the honest answer is “something that didn’t exist when I wrote this,” the control needs rebuilding — even though it hasn’t failed yet.

I gave a talk in 2021 with ten tips in it. Five years later, one statistic was false, two of the tips would get someone hurt if followed as written, and three things I never mentioned now belong near the top of the list.

That’s not a bad hit rate for security advice. It’s a completely normal one. Which is the point.
