Protecting Your Business from Cyber Threats: Practical Steps Every Leader Must Take

By Michael Murphy

Cyber attacks are no longer a distant threat reserved for large enterprises. They are a daily reality for businesses of every size, and the consequences can be existential. In this article I want to give you clear education and immediately actionable steps you can take to reduce your risk. The goal is simple: stop being an easy target.

The Reality We Face

Cyber attacks continue to grow in both volume and sophistication. The shift to remote work during the pandemic accelerated the problem, giving attackers more time and more entry points. In 2020 alone, more data records were lost than in the previous seven years combined.

Ransomware damage alone reached an estimated $20 billion that year, and infection rates have climbed steadily for more than a decade. The trend has not slowed. It is accelerating.

High-profile incidents make headlines, but they represent only a fraction of the damage. The Colonial Pipeline attack resulted in a $4.4 million ransom payment after attackers gained access through a compromised username and password belonging to a former employee whose account had never been disabled. JBS paid $11 million. Twenty-two Texas cities were forced back to paper systems after their networks were locked and data held for ransom. Many of those digital records are gone forever.

For every incident that reaches the news, hundreds more never do. Small and mid-sized businesses are frequent targets precisely because attackers know many of them lack mature defenses.

You Are Not Too Small

The most dangerous mindset is believing you have nothing worth stealing. Your reputation is valuable. Your customer relationships are valuable. Your operational data is valuable. Attackers routinely impersonate small businesses to reach the larger organizations those businesses serve. When your email account is compromised, the people who trust you become the next victims.

Small businesses account for 43 percent of all cyber attacks. Sixty percent of small companies that suffer a significant cyber incident go out of business within six months. Those are not abstract statistics. They are business-ending events that happen every week.

The first step in any recovery program is admitting you have a problem. The second is deciding you will not be a victim.

Ten Practical Tips to Strengthen Your Defenses

1. Treat Cybersecurity as a Team Sport

Data security does not begin in the IT department. It begins with leadership. As Jared Ho of the FTC has noted, “Contrary to popular belief, data security begins with the Board of Directors, not the IT Department.” Chris Riley of SSH Communications Security puts it equally clearly: securing the network is no longer someone else’s job. Executives must work together to keep digital assets safe.

As a leader you need a working understanding of the risks your company faces. Make cybersecurity a standing strategic focus rather than a technical afterthought.

2. Train and Test Your People Continuously

People remain the largest vulnerability in most organizations. More than 92 percent of malware is delivered by email. If your staff cannot reliably identify a phishing attempt, the rest of your technical controls are fighting an uphill battle.

Regular training and testing are non-negotiable. Go further than annual awareness videos. Conduct realistic phishing simulations. Establish clear policies about how financial and sensitive information is handled. One simple policy I recommend to every client: leadership will never request wire transfers, gift cards, or account changes solely by email. Those conversations always happen by phone or in person. Make that expectation explicit so employees feel empowered to question unusual requests.

When you return to the office, ask any employee the fastest way to determine whether an email is legitimate. Their answers will tell you where training is still needed.

3. Enable Multi-Factor Authentication Everywhere Possible

A password alone is no longer sufficient. Multi-factor authentication (MFA) adds a second layer—typically something you have, such as a phone or hardware token. Even if credentials are stolen, the attacker still needs the second factor.

Enable MFA on email, banking, payroll, cloud services, and any other system that holds sensitive data. The technology is mature and straightforward. In most cases the user simply receives a push notification or one-time code after entering a password.

I have seen the value of this control repeatedly. After enabling login monitoring on an executive’s mailbox, we observed dozens of attempted logins from locations around the world over a single weekend. The executive had no international offices. Those attempts were automated systems testing credentials exposed in prior breaches. Without MFA, a single successful password guess would have given the attackers full control of the account within seconds. With MFA in place, every attempt was blocked.

4. Backup, Backup, Backup—and Test the Restores

Ransomware succeeds when organizations cannot recover their own data. Maintain regular backups of systems, configurations, and critical files. Store at least one copy offline or in immutable storage so an attacker who gains network access cannot also encrypt or delete the backups.

Encryption of backups, both in transit and at rest, is essential. Equally important is regular testing. Write down the last time you performed a successful restore of a critical file. If you cannot remember the date, schedule a test this week.

5. Improve Password Hygiene

Assume many of your credentials have already been exposed. Major breaches at LinkedIn, Marriott, Target, Equifax, Facebook and countless other organizations have placed email addresses and passwords into criminal hands. Reusing the same password across sites multiplies the damage.

Require strong, unique passwords. Deploy a reputable password manager so employees are not forced to choose weak or repeated credentials. Consider email aliases (the “plus addressing” supported by Google and Microsoft 365) so a compromise at one vendor does not automatically expose your primary address.

Current guidance has moved away from forced 30-day password changes. Long, memorable passphrases that are unique to each service are more effective and less likely to be written on sticky notes.

6. Patch and Update Systems Promptly

Known vulnerabilities are among the easiest paths for attackers. Maintain a complete inventory of every device on the network—computers, printers, cameras, phones, routers, IoT devices—and keep them current. Unsupported operating systems such as Windows XP or Windows 7 have no place in a modern environment.

Replace default credentials on every device. Restrict internet access for systems that have no legitimate need for it. Forgotten devices with default passwords remain one of the most common quiet entry points.

7. Conduct a Third-Party Risk Assessment

You cannot protect what you do not know is exposed. A professional risk assessment or penetration test performed by an independent party frequently reveals issues that internal teams overlook. In one engagement with an entertainment venue we discovered thousands of Social Security numbers stored in unprotected Excel files on a personal Dropbox account—data collected for tax reporting that no one realized was still sitting in an insecure location years later.

8. Protect Endpoints Beyond Traditional Antivirus

Signature-based antivirus is no longer sufficient. Seventy-four percent of zero-day threats are not detected by traditional AV. Deploy solutions that actively hunt for malicious behavior, enable anti-ransomware controls, and consider a zero-trust model in which nothing runs unless it has been explicitly allowed. Ring-fencing applications so they can only perform their intended functions further limits lateral movement if a single system is compromised.

9. Encrypt Sensitive Data

Encrypted data is far less useful to an attacker if it is stolen. Encrypt laptops, external drives, and backups. Where regulations or good practice require it—financial services, healthcare, legal, government contracting—use email encryption for confidential information. Even when not mandated, encrypted email is a low-cost way to reduce exposure.

10. Get Qualified Help

Few organizations have the internal bandwidth or specialized expertise to address every layer of modern cybersecurity. Start with a professional assessment so you know where to focus limited resources. Review the tips above and choose at least one to implement thoroughly in the next thirty days. Speak with your critical software vendors about the security features already available in the tools you use and confirm you are taking full advantage of them.

Final Thoughts

There are two types of companies: those that have been hacked and those that are about to be hacked. We can no longer pretend that perfect prevention is possible. What we can do is raise the cost and difficulty for attackers, limit the blast radius when something does get through, and ensure we have the technical and insurance controls in place to recover.

Cyber liability insurance is not optional. General liability policies almost never cover cyber incidents. The right policy can fund forensic investigation, legal response, notification costs, business interruption, and extortion payments.

The work is continuous, but it is manageable when approached systematically. Pick one area this week. Make progress. Then move to the next.
