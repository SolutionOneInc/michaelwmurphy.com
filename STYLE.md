# Writing style rules for michaelwmurphy.com

This site is a personal brand site. The writing needs to read as a person's
voice, not as AI-generated copy. Apply these rules to any new page, article,
or copy edit, and to any AI-assisted draft before it's used.

## Hard rules (non-negotiable)

- **No em dashes (—).** Zero, anywhere in prose. See "How to fix a dash" below.
- **No en dashes (–).** Use a hyphen for compounds and ranges: "Dallas-Fort
  Worth", "2025-26", "pages 10-12".
- **US English spelling.** color, organize, analyze, defense (not colour,
  organise, analyse, defence).
- **Oxford comma, always.**

### Exceptions to the dash rule

- Quoted material, pull quotes (`<blockquote class="quote">`), testimonials,
  or anything attributed to another person or publication keep their
  original punctuation as written, even if that includes a dash. Don't
  edit inside a quote.
- The em dash before the attribution on the homepage's "Technology should
  make..." pull quote (`— Michael Murphy`) is an explicitly approved
  exception.

## How to fix a dash

Don't mechanically replace every em dash with " - ". A spaced hyphen is
just as recognizable an AI tell, and often worse. Read the sentence and
pick the right fix:

- Parenthetical aside → parentheses, or commas if the aside is short.
- Two independent clauses joined for rhythm → semicolon, or split into
  two sentences.
- Dash introducing a list or explanation → colon.
- Dash used for dramatic emphasis before a final phrase → usually just
  delete it and let the sentence run, or start a new sentence.
- Appositive ("X — one of the largest firms in the world — filed...")
  → commas, or restructure into two sentences.

If a sentence has two em dashes doing bracket duty, parentheses or commas
are almost always correct. If a sentence needs a dash to survive, rewrite
the sentence.

Vary the fix. If a paragraph ends up with three semicolons, that's its own
tell just like the dashes were. Don't lean on any single substitution.

## Vocabulary blocklist

Avoid these unless there's no natural alternative. A single instance can
be fine; a pattern across a piece is the problem.

delve, leverage (as a verb, e.g., "leverage this tool to..."), robust,
seamless, landscape, realm, testament, underscore, crucial, vital,
navigate the complexities, in today's fast-paced world, ever-evolving,
game-changer, unlock, empower, elevate, at the end of the day.

Note: "leverage" as a plain noun (business leverage, mechanical leverage)
is fine, it's the vague corporate-verb usage that's the tell.

## Phrasing tells to avoid

- "It's not just X, it's Y" and "isn't about X, it's about Y" constructions.
- "Here's the thing", "The reality is", "Let's be clear", "Make no mistake".
- "It's worth noting that", "It's important to note that", "That said"
  used as filler.
- "Whether you're a ___ or a ___" openers.
- Transitional adverbs stacked at paragraph starts: Moreover, Furthermore,
  Additionally, Consequently.
- Closing paragraphs that restate everything already said: "In conclusion",
  "At the end of the day", "The bottom line is". Cut them or say something
  new.

## Structural tells to avoid

- Rule-of-three triads repeated across a piece ("faster, smarter, and
  more secure").
- One-line fragment paragraphs used for emphasis. A couple across a long
  piece is fine and intentional. Six is a tic.
- A rhetorical question immediately followed by its own answer, used more
  than once or twice in a piece.
- Bulleted lists where every bullet is the same length and grammatical
  shape. Break the symmetry, or convert to prose.
- Bold used mid-sentence for emphasis rather than for genuine mini-headers
  or key terms. A bold lead-in that labels a distinct point (like this
  site's "**Adoption already happened.** ...") is fine. Bolding random
  words for emphasis is not.
- Headers that are all the same grammatical shape (all gerunds, all
  questions) across one piece.

## What never changes for style

- Quoted material, pull quotes, testimonials, or anything attributed to
  someone else.
- Code blocks, inline code, URLs, slugs, file paths, frontmatter keys.
- Technical claims, statistics, dates, names, and citations. If a
  punctuation fix would risk changing meaning, leave it and flag it
  instead of guessing.
- The difference between a conversational piece and a formal one. Don't
  homogenize voice across pieces that are deliberately different.
